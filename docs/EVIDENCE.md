# Evidence Base for a Bodyweight Home Calisthenics Programme

Last researched: October 2026.

## Disclaimer

This document is not medical advice. It summarises published research and guidelines to inform the design of a personal training programme. The person this programme is for reports knee issues and shoulder/wrist issues with no specific diagnosis. **A physiotherapist (or sports medicine doctor) assessment is advised before starting**, mainly for the knee and the shoulder/wrist. That assessment can identify what is causing the symptoms, rule out conditions that need a different approach, and adjust the exercise choices and pain limits below. The pain-monitoring rules in this document come from rehabilitation research, but they do not replace individual clinical advice.

Figures marked **(verify)** are ones the author could not fully confirm against the primary source (for example, only an abstract or a secondary summary was available). Check the original paper before relying on them.

## Scope and constraints

- **Person:** adult returning to training (has trained before; can do roughly 5–15 full push-ups). Has knee issues and shoulder/wrist issues, with no diagnosis. Goals: strength, flexibility/mobility, weight loss.
- **Format:** short calisthenics sessions every day (about 15–20 min), plus cycling for cardio, mostly indoors on a home trainer and sometimes outdoors.
- **Equipment:** exercise mat only. No weights, bands, bar, rings, or table/door rows. A wall is assumed to be available (wall push-ups, wall sits, balance support).
- **Back training:** the person chose floor-only substitutes for pulling (prone Y/T/W raises, reverse snow angels, Superman/prone holds, prone swimmers and similar).

## 1. Physical activity guidelines

### 1.1 WHO 2020 guidelines

- **Source:** Bull FC, Al-Ansari SS, Biddle S, et al. *World Health Organization 2020 guidelines on physical activity and sedentary behaviour.* British Journal of Sports Medicine, 2020;54(24):1451–1462. DOI: [10.1136/bjsports-2020-102955](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2020-102955). Open access: <https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/54/24/1451>
- **Aerobic (adults 18–64):** 150–300 min/week of moderate-intensity aerobic activity, or 75–150 min/week of vigorous-intensity activity, or an equivalent combination. Going beyond 300 min/week of moderate activity gives additional health benefits.
- **Muscle strengthening:** moderate or greater intensity, working all major muscle groups, on 2 or more days per week.
- **Sedentary behaviour:** reduce sitting and replace it with activity of any intensity. The evidence was not strong enough to set a numeric threshold.
- **Implication:** the cycling (indoor and outdoor) can cover the aerobic target. Daily calisthenics sessions need to cover each major muscle group at least twice per week.

### 1.2 ACSM position stands on resistance training

- **2009 stand (now superseded):** Ratamess NA, Alvar BA, Evetoch TK, Housh TJ, Kibler WB, Kraemer WJ, Triplett NT. *American College of Sports Medicine position stand. Progression models in resistance training for healthy adults.* Medicine & Science in Sports & Exercise, 2009;41(3):687–708. DOI: [10.1249/MSS.0b013e3181915670](https://doi.org/10.1249/MSS.0b013e3181915670)
  - Novices: loads of about 8–12 RM. Intermediate/advanced: 1–12 RM, periodised.
  - Load progression: increase load by about 2–10% when the person can do 1–2 reps more than the target on two consecutive sessions (the "2-for-2" rule). (verify the exact wording)
  - Frequency: 2–3 days/week for novices, rising to 4–5 for advanced.
  - Local muscular endurance: light to moderate loads, high reps, short rests.
- **2026 stand (current):** Phillips SM (chair), Currier BS, D'Souza AC, Fiatarone Singh MA, Lowisz CV, Rawson ES, Schoenfeld BJ, Smith-Ryan AE, Steen JP, Thomas GA, Triplett NT, Washington TA, Werner TJ. *Resistance Training Prescription for Muscle Function, Hypertrophy, and Physical Performance in Healthy Adults: An Overview of Reviews.* Medicine & Science in Sports & Exercise, 2026 (issue date and pages: verify). ACSM summary: <https://acsm.org/science-spotlight-acsm-releases-new-position-stand-on-resistance-training/>
  - It is an umbrella review of 137 systematic reviews covering more than 30,000 participants (verify).
  - Main messages as reported by ACSM and press coverage: any resistance training is much better than none; train all major muscle groups at least twice per week; individualise the programme to goals and enjoyment; adherence matters most. Training to failure, frequent exercise variety, and separate "beginner" programmes were reported as not necessary (verify against the full text).
  - Only the announcement and secondary coverage were available, so specific numbers from this stand are not used in this document.

### 1.3 ACSM position stand on flexibility

- **Source:** Garber CE, Blissmer B, Deschenes MR, et al. *American College of Sports Medicine position stand. Quantity and quality of exercise for developing and maintaining cardiorespiratory, musculoskeletal, and neuromotor fitness in apparently healthy adults: guidance for prescribing exercise.* Medicine & Science in Sports & Exercise, 2011;43(7):1334–1359. DOI: [10.1249/MSS.0b013e318213fefb](https://doi.org/10.1249/MSS.0b013e318213fefb)
- **Flexibility recommendations** (from Table 3, read via secondary reproductions; verify):
  - At least 2–3 days/week, with greater gains if done daily.
  - Stretch to the point of feeling tightness or slight discomfort.
  - Hold static stretches for 10–30 s for most adults, or 30–60 s for older adults.
  - Do 2–4 repetitions per stretch, adding up to about **60 s total per muscle group**.
  - Static, dynamic and PNF methods are all acceptable.
  - The paper notes that a link between flexibility and lower injury risk has not been established.

## 2. Resistance training dose

### 2.1 Weekly sets per muscle

- **Schoenfeld BJ, Ogborn D, Krieger JW.** *Dose-response relationship between weekly resistance training volume and increases in muscle mass: a systematic review and meta-analysis.* Journal of Sports Sciences, 2017;35(11):1073–1082. DOI: [10.1080/02640414.2016.1210197](https://doi.org/10.1080/02640414.2016.1210197)
  - 15 studies, 34 treatment groups. Each extra weekly set was linked to a slightly larger hypertrophy effect (about +0.37% gain per set; verify).
  - The categories <5, 5–9 and ≥10 sets/week per muscle showed only a trend (P = 0.074), favouring ≥10.
  - Data above about 10 sets/week were limited, and most participants were untrained.
- **Pelland JC, Remmert JF, Robinson ZP, Hinson SR, Zourdos MC.** *The resistance training dose-response: meta-regressions exploring the effects of weekly volume and frequency on muscle hypertrophy and strength gain.* SportRxiv preprint, 2024. Journal version reported as Sports Medicine, 2025/2026 (verify). <https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/view/460>
  - 67 studies, about 2,058 participants. Gains rose with more weekly volume but with diminishing returns, which were stronger for strength than for hypertrophy.
- **Practical reading for this person:** about **4–10 hard sets per muscle group per week** is a realistic, evidence-consistent target for a returning trainee doing 15–20 minute sessions. The low end still produces meaningful gains. More volume helps hypertrophy, but returns diminish.

### 2.2 Rep ranges and load (relevant to bodyweight)

- **Schoenfeld BJ, Grgic J, Ogborn D, Krieger JW.** *Strength and hypertrophy adaptations between low- vs. high-load resistance training: a systematic review and meta-analysis.* Journal of Strength and Conditioning Research, 2017;31(12):3508–3523. DOI: [10.1519/JSC.0000000000002200](https://doi.org/10.1519/JSC.0000000000002200)
  - Low loads (≤60% 1RM) taken to failure produced similar hypertrophy to high loads (>60% 1RM).
  - Maximal strength (1RM) gains were greater with high loads.
  - **Implication:** bodyweight exercises with high reps can build muscle if sets are taken close to failure. For strength, progress to harder leverage variations so that sets stay in a lower rep range.
- **Lasevicius T, Ugrinowitsch C, Schoenfeld BJ, et al.** *Effects of different intensities of resistance training with equated volume load on muscle strength and hypertrophy.* European Journal of Sport Science, 2018;18(6):772–780. DOI: [10.1080/17461391.2018.1450898](https://doi.org/10.1080/17461391.2018.1450898)
  - Reported via secondary summary: very low loads (20% 1RM) produced less hypertrophy than 40–80% 1RM (verify).
  - **Implication:** when an easy variation allows more than about 25–30 reps, it is probably too light to be efficient. Move to a harder variation.
- **Bodyweight-specific evidence:**
  - **Kotarsky CJ, Christensen BK, Miller JS, Hackney KJ.** *Effect of progressive calisthenic push-up training on muscle strength and thickness.* Journal of Strength and Conditioning Research, 2018;32(3):651–659 (verify pages). PubMed: <https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29466268/>. Over 4 weeks, progressing push-up variations (standard → close-grip → uneven → archer → one-arm) improved bench press 1RM and muscle thickness similarly to bench press training. Small sample, short duration.
  - **Calatayud J, Borreani S, Colado JC, et al.** *Bench press and push-up at comparable levels of muscle activity results in similar strength gains.* Journal of Strength and Conditioning Research, 2015;29(1):246–253. DOI: [10.1519/JSC.0000000000000589](https://doi.org/10.1519/JSC.0000000000000589). Banded push-ups were used, but this supports the general principle that push-ups at a matched effort produce strength gains.

### 2.3 Proximity to failure (reps in reserve)

- **Grgic J, Schoenfeld BJ, Orazem J, Sabol F.** *Effects of resistance training performed to repetition failure or non-failure on muscular strength and hypertrophy: a systematic review and meta-analysis.* Journal of Sport and Health Science, 2022;11(2):202–211. DOI: [10.1016/j.jshs.2021.01.007](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jshs.2021.01.007)
  - When volume was equated, there was no significant difference in hypertrophy or strength between training to failure and stopping short of failure.
- **Refalo MC, Helms ER, Trexler ET, Hamilton DL, Fyfe JJ.** *Influence of resistance training proximity-to-failure on skeletal muscle hypertrophy: a systematic review with meta-analysis.* Sports Medicine, 2023;53(3):649–665. DOI: [10.1007/s40279-022-01784-y](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-022-01784-y). Open access: <https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9935748/>
  - No evidence that failure training is superior. Hypertrophy appears to increase non-linearly as sets get closer to failure.
- **Robinson ZP, Pelland JC, Remmert JF, Refalo MC, Jukic I, Steele J, Zourdos MC.** *Exploring the dose–response relationship between estimated resistance training proximity to failure, strength gain, and muscle hypertrophy: a series of meta-regressions.* Sports Medicine, 2024 (volume, pages and DOI: verify on the publisher's site). Institutional record: <https://rke.abertay.ac.uk/en/publications/exploring-the-dose-response-relationship-between-estimated-resist/>. Preprint: <https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/view/295>
  - Hypertrophy gains appeared to increase as sets got closer to failure (lower RIR). Strength gains were largely unaffected by RIR across the range studied. RIR values in the included studies were estimated, not reported.
- **Halperin I, Malleron T, Har-Nir I, et al.** *Accuracy in predicting repetitions to task failure in resistance exercise: a scoping review and exploratory meta-analysis.* Sports Medicine, 2022;52(2):377–390. DOI: [10.1007/s40279-021-01559-x](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-021-01559-x)
  - People underestimated their remaining reps by about 1 rep on average (0.95; 95% CI 0.17–1.73), with large variation between studies.
  - Predictions were more accurate closer to failure and in sets with fewer total reps. Accuracy was worse for sets above about 12 reps.
  - **Implication:** for high-rep bodyweight sets, RIR estimates will be imprecise. An occasional calibration set taken to technical failure (on joint-safe exercises only) helps.

### 2.4 Frequency

- **Schoenfeld BJ, Grgic J, Krieger J.** *How many times per week should a muscle be trained to maximize muscle hypertrophy? A systematic review and meta-analysis of studies examining the effects of resistance training frequency.* Journal of Sports Sciences, 2019;37(11):1286–1295. DOI: [10.1080/02640414.2018.1555906](https://doi.org/10.1080/02640414.2018.1555906)
  - 25 studies. When weekly volume was equated, frequency did not meaningfully affect hypertrophy. Higher frequency helped when it added volume.
- **Implication for daily short sessions:** splitting the weekly volume across several short sessions is a valid way to reach the target. What matters is total hard sets per muscle per week, with each muscle trained at least twice a week (WHO 2020; ACSM 2026).

## 3. Progression models for bodyweight training

### 3.1 Double progression

- **Principle:** set a rep range (for example 8–12). Add reps session by session until every set reaches the top of the range at the target effort. Then increase the difficulty (with weights this means load; with bodyweight it means a harder variation) and restart at the bottom of the range.
- **Basis:** this is the bodyweight equivalent of the ACSM 2009 rule of increasing load once the person exceeds the target reps. The r/bodyweightfitness Recommended Routine uses the same model (3 sets of 5–8 reps; move to the next variation at 3×8). No trial compares double progression with other progression schemes in bodyweight training, so this rests on principle and practice rather than direct comparative evidence.

### 3.2 Variation (leverage) progressions

Without external load, difficulty is changed through:

1. **Leverage / body angle:** wall → knees → full push-up; plank from knees → full plank; bent-knee → extended-leg dead bug.
2. **Unilateral loading:** glute bridge → single-leg bridge; squat → split squat.
3. **Range of motion:** partial-depth squat → deeper squat (within pain limits).
4. **Tempo and pauses:** a 3-s lowering phase, or a 1–3 s pause at the hardest point, increases time under tension and difficulty without extra joint load.
5. **Hold duration:** for isometrics, increase the hold time and then move to a harder position.
6. **Eccentric-only reps:** lower slowly through the harder variation and return using an easier route (for example, lower a full push-up over 3–5 s and push up from the knees). This bridges gaps between ladder steps.

Kotarsky et al. (2018) is the main controlled evidence that progressing push-up variations produces strength gains similar to adding external load. The evidence is short-term and in young men.

### 3.3 Auto-regulation with RIR

- **Zourdos MC, Klemp A, Dolan C, et al.** *Novel resistance training-specific rating of perceived exertion scale measuring repetitions in reserve.* Journal of Strength and Conditioning Research, 2016;30(1):267–275. DOI: [10.1519/JSC.0000000000001049](https://doi.org/10.1519/JSC.0000000000001049)
- **Helms ER, Cronin J, Storey A, Zourdos MC.** *Application of the repetitions in reserve-based rating of perceived exertion scale for resistance training.* Strength and Conditioning Journal, 2016;38(4):42–49. DOI: [10.1519/SSC.0000000000000218](https://doi.org/10.1519/SSC.0000000000000218)
- **Scale:** RPE 10 = 0 RIR (no more reps possible), RPE 9 = 1 RIR, RPE 8 = 2 RIR, and so on.
- **Use:** after each set, the person records how many more good-form reps they could have done. The engine uses this to decide whether to progress, hold, or regress (see the programme rules below).
- **Limitation:** accuracy is lower for high-rep sets and in less experienced lifters (Halperin et al., 2022).

## 4. Flexibility and mobility

### 4.1 Static stretching dose

- **Thomas E, Bianco A, Paoli A, Palma A.** *The relation between stretching typology and stretching duration: the effects on range of motion.* International Journal of Sports Medicine, 2018;39(4):243–254. DOI: [10.1055/s-0044-101146](https://doi.org/10.1055/s-0044-101146)
  - Review of 23 articles. All stretching types improved range of motion (ROM) over the long term, and static stretching produced larger gains than ballistic or PNF.
  - Total weekly stretching time mattered: at least about **5 min/week** was associated with gains, while time per session did not appear to matter.
  - Stretching on more days per week was associated with larger gains. The authors suggest stretching at least 5 days/week.
  - The abstract does not clearly state whether the 5 min/week applies per muscle group or in total (verify). This document treats it as per target muscle group/region, which is the more conservative reading.
- **ACSM (Garber et al., 2011):** about 60 s total per muscle group per session, at least 2–3 days/week, and ideally daily.

### 4.2 Stretching around strength sessions

- **Behm DG, Blazevich AJ, Kay AD, McHugh M.** *Acute effects of muscle stretching on physical performance, range of motion, and injury incidence in healthy active individuals: a systematic review.* Applied Physiology, Nutrition, and Metabolism, 2016;41(1):1–11. DOI: [10.1139/apnm-2015-0235](https://doi.org/10.1139/apnm-2015-0235)
  - Static stretching right before performance caused small decrements, and these were larger with ≥60 s per muscle group (about −4.6%) than with <60 s (about −1.1%).
  - The effects were small and were reduced when dynamic activity followed the stretching.
  - **Implication:** keep pre-session stretching short and dynamic. Put the longer static stretches at the end of the session or in a dedicated mobility session.

### 4.3 Resistance training as a flexibility intervention

- **Afonso J, Ramirez-Campillo R, Moscão J, et al.** *Strength training versus stretching for improving range of motion: a systematic review and meta-analysis.* Healthcare (Basel), 2021;9(4):427. DOI: [10.3390/healthcare9040427](https://doi.org/10.3390/healthcare9040427)
  - 11 RCTs, 452 participants. There was no significant difference in ROM gains between full-range strength training and stretching (ES −0.22; 95% CI −0.55 to 0.12).
  - There was substantial heterogeneity between studies.
  - **Implication:** full-range bodyweight exercises (for example deep, controlled lunges, or prone Y raises at end range) also contribute to mobility. Dedicated stretching remains useful for specific stiff regions and is low-risk and low-cost. For this person, though, full range must stay within pain limits.

### 4.4 Mobility

"Mobility" usually means active control through a joint's range, rather than passive flexibility. Direct trial evidence for specific mobility drills is thin. The most defensible approach combines (a) full-range strength work, (b) static stretching at the doses above, and (c) dynamic drills during the warm-up.

## 5. Weight loss

### 5.1 Exercise alone compared with diet

- **Donnelly JE, Blair SN, Jakicic JM, Manore MM, Rankin JW, Smith BK.** *American College of Sports Medicine position stand. Appropriate physical activity intervention strategies for weight loss and prevention of weight regain for adults.* Medicine & Science in Sports & Exercise, 2009;41(2):459–471. DOI: [10.1249/MSS.0b013e3181949333](https://doi.org/10.1249/MSS.0b013e3181949333)
  - 150–250 min/week of moderate activity helps prevent weight gain but gives only modest weight loss.
  - More than 250 min/week has been linked to clinically significant weight loss.
  - Exercise alone at about 150 min/week was associated with roughly 2–3 kg of loss (verify).
  - Resistance training alone does not produce clinically significant weight loss, but it can increase fat-free mass.
- **Swift DL, Johannsen NM, Lavie CJ, Earnest CP, Church TS.** *The role of exercise and physical activity in weight loss and maintenance.* Progress in Cardiovascular Diseases, 2014;56(4):441–447. DOI: [10.1016/j.pcad.2013.09.012](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.pcad.2013.09.012) (narrative review). Unless aerobic exercise volume is very high, clinically significant weight loss from exercise alone is unlikely. Exercise helps limit weight regain.
- **Bellicha A, van Baak MA, Battista F, et al.** *Effect of exercise training on weight loss, body composition changes, and weight maintenance in adults with overweight or obesity: an overview of 12 systematic reviews and 149 studies.* Obesity Reviews, 2021;22(Suppl 4):e13256. DOI: [10.1111/obr.13256](https://doi.org/10.1111/obr.13256)
  - Exercise alone gave mean weight losses of about 1.5–3.5 kg and fat losses of about 1.3–2.6 kg, and reduced visceral fat.
  - Resistance training limited lean mass loss during weight loss (about 0.8 kg difference).
- **Swift DL, et al. (on behalf of the American Heart Association).** *Role of Physical Activity in Obesity Treatment and Cardiometabolic Health: A Scientific Statement.* Circulation, 2026. DOI: [10.1161/CIR.0000000000001441](https://doi.org/10.1161/CIR.0000000000001441) (verify details)
  - Physical activity alone is unlikely to produce ≥5% weight loss unless aerobic volumes are very high.
  - It improves blood pressure, insulin resistance and lipids independently of weight loss.
  - About 200–300 min/week of moderate activity is associated with long-term weight-loss maintenance (verify).
- **Implication:** an energy deficit created through diet drives weight loss. Cycling contributes energy expenditure and helps maintenance. The calisthenics programme's main role in weight loss is to **preserve muscle and strength** while the person is in a deficit. The programme should not promise weight loss from the calisthenics sessions themselves.

### 5.2 Resistance training to preserve lean mass in a deficit

- **Sardeli AV, Komatsu TR, Mori MA, Gáspari AF, Chacon-Mikahil MPT.** *Resistance training prevents muscle loss induced by caloric restriction in obese elderly individuals: a systematic review and meta-analysis.* Nutrients, 2018;10(4):423. DOI: [10.3390/nu10040423](https://doi.org/10.3390/nu10040423)
  - Six RCTs in older adults with obesity. Adding resistance training to caloric restriction preserved about 0.8 kg of lean mass (about 93.5% of the lean mass lost with restriction alone), with similar fat loss.
  - The evidence base is small and limited to older adults.
- Bellicha et al. (2021) report a similar lean mass benefit (about 0.8 kg) in adults with overweight or obesity.

### 5.3 Protein intake

- **Jäger R, Kerksick CM, Campbell BI, et al.** *International Society of Sports Nutrition position stand: protein and exercise.* Journal of the International Society of Sports Nutrition, 2017;14:20. DOI: [10.1186/s12970-017-0177-8](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12970-017-0177-8)
  - 1.4–2.0 g/kg body mass/day is sufficient for most exercising people.
  - Higher intakes (2.3–3.1 g/kg/day) may help resistance-trained, lean people preserve lean mass during caloric restriction (verify whether this applies per kg of body mass or of fat-free mass in the final text).
  - Spread intake across meals, at about 0.25 g/kg or 20–40 g per meal.
- **Morton RW, Murphy KT, McKellar SR, et al.** *A systematic review, meta-analysis and meta-regression of the effect of protein supplementation on resistance training-induced gains in muscle mass and strength in healthy adults.* British Journal of Sports Medicine, 2018;52(6):376–384. DOI: [10.1136/bjsports-2017-097608](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2017-097608)
  - 49 RCTs. Gains in fat-free mass plateaued at about 1.6 g/kg/day total protein (upper 95% CI about 2.2 g/kg/day).
- **Murphy CH, Hector AJ, Phillips SM.** *Considerations for protein intake in managing weight loss in athletes.* European Journal of Sport Science, 2015;15(1):21–28 (verify pages). PubMed: <https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25014731/>
  - During a moderate deficit with resistance training, about 1.8–2.7 g/kg/day (about 2.3–3.1 g/kg of fat-free mass) is suggested.
- **Practical range for this person:** about **1.6–2.2 g/kg/day** during a deficit. If body fat is high, base the calculation on target or adjusted body weight rather than current body weight. This last point is a common practical adjustment, not a finding of the cited papers. Any underlying kidney disease, or other condition where protein intake matters, should be discussed with a doctor or dietitian.

## 6. Knee-friendly lower body training

### 6.1 Exercise therapy for knee pain

- **Patellofemoral pain:** Collins NJ, Barton CJ, van Middelkoop M, et al. *2018 consensus statement on exercise therapy and physical interventions (orthoses, taping and manual therapy) to treat patellofemoral pain: recommendations from the 5th International Patellofemoral Pain Research Retreat, Gold Coast, Australia, 2017.* British Journal of Sports Medicine, 2018;52(18):1170–1178. DOI: [10.1136/bjsports-2018-099397](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2018-099397)
  - Exercise therapy is recommended to reduce pain and improve function.
  - **Combining hip-focused and knee-focused exercise** is preferred over knee exercise alone.
- **Lack S, Barton C, Sohan O, Crossley K, Morrissey D.** *Proximal muscle rehabilitation is effective for patellofemoral pain: a systematic review with meta-analysis.* British Journal of Sports Medicine, 2015;49(21):1365–1376. DOI: [10.1136/bjsports-2015-094723](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2015-094723)
  - Hip and trunk ("proximal") strengthening reduces pain and improves function. Combining it with quadriceps work gave better outcomes at 1 year.
- **Knee osteoarthritis (OARSI):** Bannuru RR, Osani MC, Vaysbrot EE, et al. *OARSI guidelines for the non-surgical management of knee, hip, and polyarticular osteoarthritis.* Osteoarthritis and Cartilage, 2019;27(11):1578–1589. DOI: [10.1016/j.joca.2019.06.011](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.joca.2019.06.011)
  - Core treatments for knee OA are education plus structured land-based exercise, with or without dietary weight management.
- **Knee osteoarthritis (NICE):** NICE guideline NG226. *Osteoarthritis in over 16s: diagnosis and management.* 2022. <https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng226>
  - Offer therapeutic exercise tailored to the person, such as local muscle strengthening and general aerobic fitness.
  - Explain that joint pain may increase when starting exercise, and that sticking with exercise long term brings benefits.
- **Implication:** whatever the underlying knee issue, structured strengthening of the quadriceps plus the hip (abductors, external rotators, extensors), together with weight management, is supported. The exact diagnosis changes the details, which is why a physiotherapist assessment is advised.

### 6.2 Hip strengthening exercises (mat-only)

- **Distefano LJ, Blackburn JT, Marshall SW, Padua DA.** *Gluteal muscle activation during common therapeutic exercises.* Journal of Orthopaedic & Sports Physical Therapy, 2009;39(7):532–540. DOI: [10.2519/jospt.2009.2796](https://doi.org/10.2519/jospt.2009.2796)
  - Side-lying hip abduction gave the highest gluteus medius activation of the exercises tested.
  - Single-limb squat and single-limb deadlift gave the highest gluteus maximus activation.
  - Clamshells also activated the gluteus medius. Clamshell knee angle (30° vs 60°) made little difference (verify).
- These are EMG studies in healthy people. They show which exercises target which muscles, but not clinical outcomes.

### 6.3 Isometrics

- **Rio E, Kidgell D, Purdam C, et al.** *Isometric exercise induces analgesia and reduces inhibition in patellar tendinopathy.* British Journal of Sports Medicine, 2015;49(19):1277–1283. DOI: [10.1136/bjsports-2014-094386](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2014-094386)
  - Only 6 participants. Sustained isometric quadriceps contractions (5 × 45 s at about 70% MVC; verify) reduced pain immediately, and the effect lasted at least 45 min.
- Later studies, including Holden et al. (2019, verify), had mixed results for pain relief.
- **Practical use:** isometrics such as a wall sit at a pain-tolerable knee angle are a low-skill, adjustable way to load the quadriceps when dynamic squats are painful. Their pain-relieving effect is uncertain. Their main value here is as a tolerable entry-level loading option.
- **Exclusion:** the Spanish squat needs a band or strap anchored to a fixed point, so it is excluded under the equipment constraint.

### 6.4 Squat depth

- **Escamilla RF.** *Knee biomechanics of the dynamic squat exercise.* Medicine & Science in Sports & Exercise, 2001;33(1):127–141. DOI: [10.1097/00005768-200101000-00020](https://doi.org/10.1097/00005768-200101000-00020) (verify DOI). PubMed: <https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11194098/>
  - Patellofemoral and tibiofemoral compressive forces rise with knee flexion and peak near maximum flexion.
  - The 0–50° knee flexion range produced the lowest knee forces and may suit many knee rehabilitation patients.
  - For healthy knees, parallel squats (about 0–100°) were considered acceptable.
- **Hartmann H, Wirth K, Klusemann M.** *Analysis of the load on the knee joint and vertebral column with changes in squatting depth and weight load.* Sports Medicine, 2013;43(10):993–1008. DOI: [10.1007/s40279-013-0073-6](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-013-0073-6)
  - Argued that deep squats are not inherently harmful for healthy knees when technique is correct (verify the specific conclusions).
- **Implication:** start with a partial range of about 0–50/60° of knee flexion. Add depth only while pain stays within the monitoring limits. Depth is a progression variable, not a fixed requirement.

### 6.5 Pain-monitoring model

- **Original source:** Thomeé R. *A comprehensive treatment approach for patellofemoral pain syndrome in young women.* Physical Therapy, 1997;77(12):1690–1703. DOI: [10.1093/ptj/77.12.1690](https://doi.org/10.1093/ptj/77.12.1690) (verify DOI)
- **Applied and tested in:** Silbernagel KG, Thomeé R, Eriksson BI, Karlsson J. *Continued sports activity, using a pain-monitoring model, during rehabilitation in patients with Achilles tendinopathy: a randomized controlled study.* American Journal of Sports Medicine, 2007;35(6):897–906. DOI: [10.1177/0363546506298279](https://doi.org/10.1177/0363546506298279) (verify pages)
- **The model** (as described in secondary sources citing these papers; verify exact wording):
  - Pain 0–2/10 is "safe".
  - Pain 2–5/10 is "acceptable".
  - Pain above 5/10 is "high risk".
  - Pain may reach up to 5/10 during or after exercise, but it should settle by the next morning.
  - Pain and stiffness should not increase from week to week.
- In the 2007 RCT, continuing activity under this model gave outcomes no worse than resting.
- The zone thresholds were based on clinical experience rather than formal validation (verify). Later studies used slightly different thresholds.
- The GLA:D knee/hip OA programme uses a similar rule: pain up to 5/10 is acceptable during and after sessions, provided it returns to the usual level by the next morning (verify). Programme information: <https://www.glaid.dk/> (verify URL)
- **Programme choice:** because no diagnosis is available, this document uses a more conservative threshold than the original model: **≤3/10 during exercise, settling within 24 h** (see the programme rules). This is a deliberate safety margin, not a published threshold. A physiotherapist may relax it after assessment.

## 7. Shoulder- and wrist-friendly upper body training

### 7.1 Scapular stabiliser and rotator cuff training (prone and side-lying)

- **Cools AM, Dewitte V, Lanszweert F, et al.** *Rehabilitation of scapular muscle balance: which exercises to prescribe?* American Journal of Sports Medicine, 2007;35(10):1744–1751. DOI: [10.1177/0363546507303560](https://doi.org/10.1177/0363546507303560)
  - EMG study in 45 healthy people.
  - Four exercises gave high lower and middle trapezius activation with low upper trapezius activation: **side-lying forward flexion, side-lying external rotation, prone horizontal abduction with external rotation** (the prone "T" with thumbs up), and **prone extension**.
- **Ekstrom RA, Donatelli RA, Soderberg GL.** *Surface electromyographic analysis of exercises for the trapezius and serratus anterior muscles.* Journal of Orthopaedic & Sports Physical Therapy, 2003;33(5):247–258. DOI: [10.2519/jospt.2003.33.5.247](https://doi.org/10.2519/jospt.2003.33.5.247)
  - The prone arm raise overhead in line with the lower trapezius (the prone "Y") gave the highest lower trapezius activity.
  - Prone horizontal extension with external rotation, and the prone Y, gave the highest middle trapezius activity.
  - The prone Y works above 90° of elevation, so it may be less suitable early on for painful shoulders.
- **Reinold MM, Wilk KE, Fleisig GS, et al.** *Electromyographic analysis of the rotator cuff and deltoid musculature during common shoulder external rotation exercises.* Journal of Orthopaedic & Sports Physical Therapy, 2004;34(7):385–394. DOI: [10.2519/jospt.2004.34.7.385](https://doi.org/10.2519/jospt.2004.34.7.385)
  - Side-lying external rotation gave the greatest infraspinatus (about 62% MVIC) and teres minor (about 67% MVIC) activity of the 7 exercises tested, using dumbbells.
  - Without a weight, side-lying ER gives very low load. In this programme it serves mainly as motor control and endurance work. It can be made harder with slow tempo and end-range holds, but cannot be progressed much further.
- **Exercise therapy for shoulder pain:** Naunton J, Street G, Littlewood C, Haines T, Malliaras P. *Effectiveness of progressive and resisted and non-progressive or non-resisted exercise in rotator cuff related shoulder pain: a systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials.* Clinical Rehabilitation, 2020;34(9):1198–1216 (verify). PubMed: <https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32571081/>
  - Progressive, resisted exercise had uncertain benefit (low-certainty evidence). Non-progressive, non-resisted exercise showed no significant benefit.
  - **Implication:** shoulder exercise should be progressive (in reps, holds, leverage and range), not a fixed set of easy drills repeated indefinitely.

### 7.2 Serratus anterior and the push-up plus

- **Ludewig PM, Hoff MS, Osowski EE, Meschke SA, Rundquist PJ.** *Relative balance of serratus anterior and upper trapezius muscle activity during push-up exercises.* American Journal of Sports Medicine, 2004;32(2):484–493. DOI: [10.1177/0363546503258911](https://doi.org/10.1177/0363546503258911)
  - The standard push-up plus (adding full scapular protraction at the top) gave high serratus activation and a low upper trapezius/serratus ratio.
  - Wall, knee and elbow versions gave less serratus activation and higher ratios.
  - These modified versions form a graded progression for people who cannot yet do the full version.

### 7.3 Push-up variations that reduce wrist extension

- **Polovinets O, Wolf A, Wollstein R.** *Force transmission through the wrist during performance of push-ups on a hyperextended and a neutral wrist.* Journal of Hand Therapy, 2018;31(3):322–330. DOI: [10.1016/j.jht.2017.04.005](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jht.2017.04.005)
  - 14 volunteers. The load path through the wrist differed between hyperextended (flat palm) and neutral (fist or handle) push-ups.
  - The authors concluded that push-ups on a neutral wrist are likely safer, because ligaments may be loaded preferentially in hyperextension. This is a biomechanical inference, not an outcome study.
- **Wrist-friendly options under the mat-only constraint:**
  1. Push-ups on **fists** (neutral wrist, knuckles on a folded mat).
  2. **Forearm**-based exercises (forearm plank, forearm side plank, sphinx position).
  3. **Wall/high-incline** push-ups (smaller wrist extension angle and lower load).
  4. Hands directly under the shoulders, fingers slightly turned out.
- No RCT compares fist and palm push-ups for wrist pain. This guidance is based on biomechanics and clinical convention.

### 7.4 Push and pull balance without pulling equipment

- **Kolber MJ, Beekhuizen KS, Cheng MS, Hellman MA.** *Shoulder injuries attributed to resistance training: a brief review.* Journal of Strength and Conditioning Research, 2010;24(6):1696–1704. DOI: [10.1519/JSC.0b013e3181dc4330](https://doi.org/10.1519/JSC.0b013e3181dc4330) (verify)
  - Up to about 36% of resistance training injuries involved the shoulder (verify).
  - Muscle imbalance was proposed as a risk factor, but most of the evidence is retrospective.
- **Negrete RJ, Hanney WJ, Pabian P, Kolber MJ.** *Upper body push and pull strength ratio in recreationally active adults.* International Journal of Sports Physical Therapy, 2013;8(2):138–144 (verify). PMC: <https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3625793/>
  - Reported push-up : modified pull-up ratios of about 1.57:1 in men and 2.72:1 in women, as reference values.
  - The authors said the link between these ratios and injury needs further research.
- **Assessment:** no prospective evidence establishes a "safe" push:pull ratio. The idea of balancing pushing with posterior shoulder and scapular work is a plausible, widely used clinical convention.
- **Limitation of floor-only substitutes:** prone Y/T/W raises, reverse snow angels and swimmers mainly train the lower and middle trapezius, rhomboids, posterior deltoid, external rotators and spinal extensors, all at low load. They do **not** load the latissimus dorsi or elbow flexors (biceps) the way rows or pull-ups do. Expect limited hypertrophy in those muscles. This is a known trade-off of the chosen constraint.
- **Programme convention:** match or exceed weekly pushing sets with prone and scapular sets (about 1:1 to 1:1.5 push:posterior sets). This is a heuristic, not a figure from a study.

## 8. Concurrent training with cycling

- **Wilson JM, Marin PJ, Rhea MR, Wilson SMC, Loenneke JP, Anderson JC.** *Concurrent training: a meta-analysis examining interference of aerobic and resistance exercises.* Journal of Strength and Conditioning Research, 2012;26(8):2293–2307. DOI: [10.1519/JSC.0b013e31823a3e2d](https://doi.org/10.1519/JSC.0b013e31823a3e2d)
  - 21 studies. Running performed concurrently with resistance training reduced gains in hypertrophy and strength; **cycling did not**.
  - Interference increased with the frequency and duration of endurance training.
- **Schumann M, Feuerbacher JF, Sünkeler M, et al.** *Compatibility of concurrent aerobic and strength training for skeletal muscle size and function: an updated systematic review and meta-analysis.* Sports Medicine, 2022;52(3):601–612. DOI: [10.1007/s40279-021-01587-7](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-021-01587-7). Open access: <https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8891239/>
  - Concurrent training did not compromise gains in maximal strength or hypertrophy, regardless of aerobic mode, frequency, training status, age, or whether the two types of training were done in the same session or on separate days.
  - Explosive strength gains were attenuated, especially when both types were done in the same session.
- **Implication:**
  - Interference with strength or hypertrophy gains from home cycling is likely small, especially at this person's training volume.
  - Fatigue and joint load still matter, particularly with knee issues: hard interval cycling and leg-focused calisthenics on the same day both load the quadriceps.
  - Scheduling rules (see the programme rules) therefore keep leg sessions away from hard cycling days, or order them sensibly. These scheduling rules are practical choices, not evidence-mandated.

## 9. Warm-up

- **Fradkin AJ, Zazryn TR, Smoliga JM.** *Effects of warming-up on physical performance: a systematic review with meta-analysis.* Journal of Strength and Conditioning Research, 2010;24(1):140–148. DOI: [10.1519/JSC.0b013e3181c643a0](https://doi.org/10.1519/JSC.0b013e3181c643a0)
  - About 79% of the performance criteria examined improved after a warm-up.
  - Warming up had little evidence of harm.
- **Jeffreys I.** *Warm-up revisited: the RAMP method of optimising warm-ups.* Professional Strength and Conditioning, 2007;(6):12–18 (verify). RAMP stands for:
  - **R**aise: increase body temperature and heart rate.
  - **A**ctivate and **M**obilise: activate key muscles and move key joints through their range.
  - **P**otentiate: ramp up towards the session's intensity.
  - This is a framework, not an experimentally tested protocol.
- See Behm et al. (2016) in section 4.2: long static stretches immediately before training slightly reduce performance, and the effect is smaller when dynamic activity follows.
- **Implication:** a 3–4 minute RAMP-style warm-up with dynamic movements. If the session follows a cycling session, the "Raise" phase can be skipped.

## Programme rules derived from the evidence

These rules are written so that a programme engine can apply them. Where a rule is a design choice rather than a direct finding, it is labelled *(heuristic)*.

### R1. Weekly structure

- **Session types:**
  - **A: Push + anti-extension core.** Horizontal push ladder, push-up plus or serratus work, triceps (optional), plank or dead bug ladder.
  - **B: Legs + hips (knee-friendly).** Knee-dominant ladder, hip extension ladder, hip abduction ladder, calf raises.
  - **C: Back/scapular + extension/lateral core.** Two or three prone or side-lying shoulder ladders, spinal extension ladder, side plank ladder.
  - **D: Mobility + light core.** 15–20 min of static stretching and mobility drills, plus 1–2 low-fatigue core or rotator cuff exercises.
- **Default week (7 days):** `A, B, C, D, A, B, C`. This trains each major muscle group twice a week (WHO 2020; ACSM 2026) and includes one mobility-focused day.
- **Rolling rotation:** if a day is missed, the engine continues the sequence (it does not skip ahead). A muscle group should get at least 2 sessions in any 8-day window; if not, the engine prioritises the missing session type.
- **Cycling adjustment** *(heuristic, informed by Wilson 2012 and Schumann 2022)*:
  - The user tags each cycling day as `none`, `easy` (zone 1–2, conversational), or `hard` (intervals, threshold, or longer than 60 min).
  - On a `hard` cycling day, do not schedule B. Swap it with the next A, C or D.
  - Do not schedule B on the day after a `hard` cycling day if the previous B caused next-morning knee pain above baseline.
  - If B and cycling fall on the same day, do B first or separate them by at least 3 h. Run the knee pain check after both.
  - Place D on the day of the week with the hardest cycling session, or on the day after it.
- **Aerobic target:** total cycling of 150–300 min/week of moderate intensity (or an equivalent mix with vigorous work), per WHO 2020. For weight-loss maintenance, about 200–300 min/week is associated with better outcomes (AHA 2026; Donnelly 2009).

### R2. Session timing (15–20 min)

| Block | Duration | Content |
|---|---|---|
| Warm-up (RAMP) | 3–4 min | Raise: 60–90 s of marching on the spot, step-jacks or similar low-impact movement (skip if cycling was just done). Activate/Mobilise: 4–6 dynamic drills relevant to the session, 6–10 reps each. Potentiate: 1 easy set of the first exercise at RIR ≥4. |
| Main block | 10–13 min | 3 exercises (sometimes 4), 2–3 sets each. Pair non-competing exercises (for example push + leg, or prone + core) and alternate them, resting 45–90 s between sets. |
| Cool-down stretch | 2–4 min | 2–3 static stretches, 2 × 30 s each (see R9). |

### R3. Volume targets

- **Hard sets per muscle group per week:** start at about 4–6 in weeks 1–2, then aim for about **6–10**. Do not exceed about 12 for any group within this time budget.
- **Per A/B/C session:** 6–9 working sets in total.
- **Push-to-posterior balance** *(heuristic)*: weekly prone/scapular sets should be at least equal to weekly horizontal-push sets, and ideally 1.0–1.5× as many.
- **Counting rules:**
  - Isometric holds count as 1 set per hold block at the target effort.
  - Warm-up sets do not count.
  - Mobility and stretching do not count.

### R4. Rep and hold targets per exercise class

| Class | Target range | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Strength ladders (push-ups, squats, split squats, single-leg bridges) | **6–12 reps** | When the top of the range is reached, progress the variation (R6). |
| Hypertrophy/endurance for low-load exercises (prone Y/T/W, snow angels, hip abduction, clamshells, calf raises) | **10–20 reps** | Add a 2–3 s hold at the top before moving to the next step. Above about 25 reps, progress the step (Lasevicius 2018). |
| Isometric holds (wall sit, planks, side planks, hollow, Superman) | **20–45 s** (wall sit: up to 45 s × 3–5) | Progress the hold time first, then the variation. |
| Rotator cuff and side-lying work (unloaded) | **12–20 reps** with a 2–3 s end-range hold | Mainly motor control. Progression is limited without external load. |

### R5. Effort targets (RIR)

- **Weeks 1–2 (re-entry):** RIR 3–4 on all exercises. Use this period to learn the RIR scale and calibrate the ladder starting steps.
- **From week 3:**
  - Main ladders: **RIR 1–3**, with a default target of RIR 2.
  - Exercises that load a symptomatic joint (knee for B; shoulder or wrist for push-ups and prone overhead work): **RIR 2–3**. Never go to failure on these.
  - Low-risk isolation work (glute bridges, clamshells, prone T/W, calf raises): RIR 0–2 on the last set is acceptable.
- **Calibration** *(heuristic, based on Halperin 2022)*: once every 4 weeks, take the last set of one low-risk exercise to technical failure and compare the reps achieved with the RIR the person predicted.

### R6. Progression triggers (double progression)

1. **Progress within the step:** if reps at the target RIR are below the top of the range, aim for +1 rep per set next session (or +5 s for holds).
2. **Progress to the next step:**
   - when **all working sets reach the top of the rep or hold range at RIR ≥2** in one session;
   - **and** the maximum pain during the session was ≤2/10;
   - **and** next-morning pain was at or below baseline.
   - Then move to the next ladder step and start at the bottom of the range.
3. **Bridging:** if the new step gives fewer reps than the bottom of the range at RIR 1, use a mixed approach:
   - first set on the new step (for as many reps as possible at RIR 2), remaining sets on the old step;
   - or eccentric-only reps on the new step (3–5 s lowering);
   - or a tempo or pause version of the old step.
   - Re-test after 2–3 sessions.
4. **Tempo levers** (between steps, or when the next step is contraindicated by pain): 3-s lowering → 1–2 s pause at the hardest point → 3-s lowering with pause.
5. **Range levers** (knee-dominant exercises): increase squat or lunge depth by about 10–15° (about one hand-width) only after one full session at the current depth with pain ≤2/10.

### R7. Regression and pain rules

The user reports pain from 0 to 10 for each affected region (knee, shoulder, wrist) during each exercise and the next morning.

| Signal | Action |
|---|---|
| Pain during exercise **≤2/10** | Continue as planned. |
| Pain during exercise **3/10** | Allowed. Finish the set, but do not progress that exercise next session. |
| Pain during exercise **4–5/10** | Stop the set. Switch to a variation with lower joint load (wrist: fists or forearms; shoulder: lower range, below 90° elevation; knee: shallower depth or isometric), or regress one step. Do not progress next session. |
| Pain during exercise **>5/10** | Stop the exercise for this session. Replace it with a low-load option from the same ladder or a non-painful alternative. Flag it for review. |
| **Next-morning** pain or stiffness above the usual baseline | Next time that pattern is trained, reduce its sets by one third and regress one step. If this happens 2 sessions in a row, pause that pattern for 1 week and suggest a physiotherapist review. |
| Pain or stiffness rising **week on week** (from the Thomeé model) | Hold progression for all exercises loading that joint. Reduce volume for that joint by about 30% for one week. |
| **Red flags**: swelling, locking, giving way, sharp pain, night pain, numbness/tingling, pain lasting more than 48 h, or pain after a fall or impact | Stop the programme for that region and recommend medical or physiotherapy assessment. |
| **Performance drop:** reps below the bottom of the range at the target RIR in 2 consecutive sessions (without pain) | Regress one step, or drop one set. If this happens on 3 or more exercises in the same week, trigger an early deload (R8). |

The ≤3/10 limit is stricter than the 5/10 used in the Thomeé/Silbernagel model. This is a deliberate choice because there is no diagnosis. A physiotherapist can raise the limit.

### R8. Deloads

- **Planned:** every **6th week** (within the commonly used 4–8 week range; Bell et al. 2023/2025; Rogerson et al. 2024 report an average of about 5.6 weeks). Keep the same frequency and exercises. Halve the sets (round up) and use RIR ≥4. Mobility and cycling continue, with cycling at easy intensity only.
- **Reactive:** trigger a deload early if any of the following happen:
  - (a) performance-drop rule hit on 3 or more exercises in a week;
  - (b) next-morning joint pain above baseline on 3 or more days in a week;
  - (c) the user reports poor sleep, persistent soreness, or low motivation for 5 or more days.
- After a deload, resume at the same steps with RIR 2–3 for the first week.
- **Sources:**
  - Bell L, Strafford BW, Coleman M, Androulakis Korakakis P, Nolan D. *Integrating deloading into strength and physique sports training programmes: an international Delphi consensus approach.* Sports Medicine – Open, 2023;9:87. DOI: [10.1186/s40798-023-00633-0](https://doi.org/10.1186/s40798-023-00633-0) (verify).
  - Rogerson D, Nolan D, Korakakis PA, Immonen V, Wolf M, Bell L. *Deloading practices in strength and physique sports: a cross-sectional survey.* Sports Medicine – Open, 2024;10:26. DOI: [10.1186/s40798-024-00691-y](https://doi.org/10.1186/s40798-024-00691-y).
  - Both describe practitioner practice, not controlled trials. Bell et al. 2025 (Strength and Conditioning Journal) describe the 4–8 week range as common practice (verify).

### R9. Stretching and mobility dose

- **End of every A/B/C session:** 2–3 static stretches, each **2 × 30 s** (about 60 s per muscle group; Garber 2011). Hold to mild tension, not pain. Rotate the stretches so each priority region is stretched on at least 5 days per week.
- **Weekly minimum per priority region:** **≥5 min/week** (Thomas 2018; applied per region as the conservative reading). Priority regions:
  - hip flexors;
  - hamstrings;
  - calves;
  - chest/anterior shoulder;
  - thoracic spine;
  - gluteals and hip rotators;
  - quadriceps (only if knee flexion is comfortable).
- **D session:** 15–20 min. 6–8 stretches or mobility drills, 2–3 × 30–45 s each. Add 1–2 low-fatigue exercises such as side-lying ER, dead bug or clamshell.
- **Before training:** dynamic drills only. Keep static holds under 30 s per muscle before strength work (Behm 2016).
- **Full-range strength work** also counts towards mobility (Afonso 2021). Within pain limits, prefer full-range versions of exercises.

### R10. Initial calibration (session 1–3)

- **Push:** one set of full push-ups (on fists if wrists are sensitive) to RIR 2, or to pain 3/10, whichever comes first.
  - 8–12 reps: start at **full push-up** (step P5).
  - 3–7 reps: start at **knee push-up** (P3) or eccentric full push-ups (P4).
  - <3 reps or pain: start at **wall push-up** (P1).
- **Knee:** wall sit at about 45° for up to 30 s, then a partial squat (0–50°) for 10 reps.
  - Pain ≤2/10 on both: start at **partial-range squat** (K3).
  - Otherwise start at **straight-leg raise and shallow wall sit** (K0–K1).
- **Shoulder:** prone T and prone W, 10 reps each.
  - Pain-free: include **prone Y** (S3) from week 2.
  - Pain with overhead positions: keep work below 90° (W, T, side-lying ER) until pain-free for 2 weeks.
- **Wrist:** if full-palm loading causes pain above 2/10, use fist or forearm variants by default.

### R11. Nutrition and weight-loss notes (for display, not prescription)

- Weight change is driven mostly by energy intake. The calisthenics programme should present itself as protecting muscle and strength during weight loss, not as a fat-loss tool (Donnelly 2009; Bellicha 2021; AHA 2026).
- Protein: about **1.6–2.2 g/kg/day** during a deficit, spread over 3–4 meals of 20–40 g (Morton 2018; Jäger 2017; Murphy 2015).
- Suggest that the user consult a doctor or registered dietitian for an individual energy target, particularly if they have medical conditions.


### R12. Variation rotation

**Purpose:** give each emphasis (hand width, stance, hip angle and so on) exposure about once every 2–3 weeks, without breaking double progression.

**Evidence:**
- Kassiano 2022 (8 studies): systematic variation may help regional hypertrophy and strength, while excessive, random variation may hinder gains.
- Fonseca 2014: varying exercises gave hypertrophy in all four quadriceps heads.
- Baz-Valle 2019: random daily variation gave similar gains and better intrinsic motivation in trained men.
- ACSM 2026 reported that frequent variation is not necessary (verify).

**Status:** variation is therefore an optional emphasis and adherence tool, not a requirement. The mechanics below are *(heuristic)*.

1. **Progression belongs to the step.** `ladder_progress` stores the step, as now. A variation shares the step's rep or hold range, RIR target, set count and rest. It never has a separate step.
2. **Which sets count:**
   - **Step-up (R6.2 and G1):** use sets on the standard version and on variations rated "similar" or "harder".
   - **R6.1 suggested reps:** use the last session on the **same version** within 6 weeks, plus 1 rep (or +5 s). If there is none, use the last standard result minus 2 reps (minus 10 s for holds), and not below the bottom of the range.
   - **R7 pain rules:** apply to every version.
   - **R7 performance-drop rule:** applies only to the standard version and "similar" variations.
3. **Rotation pattern per ladder:** let *n* be the number of completed sessions on the current step.
   - For the first 2 sessions on a step (n < 2), use the standard version.
   - After that, alternate: **standard on every other exposure**, and variations in round-robin order on the remaining exposures (S, V1, S, V2, S, V3 …).
   - Use at most **3 active variations** per step. If more exist, pick by the priority list for that step (the table order above).
   - **Ladders in a fixed slot** are trained about twice a week (A, B and C each occur twice in R1), so each variation returns about every 2 weeks (2 variations) or 3 weeks (3 variations).
   - **Ladders in an alternating slot** (for example `['plank', 'deadbug', 'crawl']`) are trained about once a week. They use at most **1** active variation (S, V1), which returns every 2 weeks.
4. **When to use the standard version only:**
   - weeks 1–2 (re-entry) and calibration sessions (R10);
   - deload weeks and the first week after a deload (R8);
   - the session after any R7 event on that ladder;
   - any bridging session (R6.3).
5. **Skipping a variation for joint reasons:** skip a variation, and use the standard version instead, when its load on a region (Knee, Wrist, Shoulder columns) is higher than the standard's and that region had any of the following:
   - pain ≥3/10 in the last 2 exposures of any exercise loading it;
   - a "worse" morning check in the last 7 days;
   - an active R7 pause.
6. **Disabling a variation:** if a variation itself gives pain ≥4/10, or is followed by a "worse" morning twice, disable that variation for 4 weeks. R7 still decides any change to the ladder step.
7. **Same version all session:** the warm-up "potentiate" set uses the same version as the working sets that day.
8. **Advanced steps:** an advanced step's own variations, if added later, follow the same rule. X ladder steps have no variations for now.
10. **Choosing a variation by hand:** the workout screen lets the user switch to another variation of the same step for that session (user's choice, October 2026: rotate and swap). This does not change the step.
9. **Data:** each variation is an `Exercise` with its own ID (for example `P5n`), animation, cues and `regions`, plus:
   - `variationOf` (step ID);
   - `difficulty` (`'similar' | 'harder' | 'easier'`);
   - `priority`.

   Sets are logged under the variation's ID. The engine maps them to the step for progression.

### R13. Unlocking advanced steps

The rules extend R6 (which needs **one** session at the top of the range at RIR ≥2) and R7. Advanced steps add more load on a sensitive joint, coordination or balance demand, so they use a stricter, longer window. All thresholds are *(heuristic)*.

#### R13.1 General unlock gate (all advanced steps and all X steps)

The engine offers an advanced step only when all of G1–G6 hold. Each condition uses logged data: sets (reps or seconds, RIR, pain), morning check-ins, ladder steps and programme week.

| Rule | Condition |
|---|---|
| **G1. Mastery of the previous step** | In **2 of the last 3** sessions on the prerequisite step, all working sets reached the top of the rep or hold range at **RIR ≥2**. Sets on variations count only if their difficulty is "similar" or "harder" (R12). |
| **G2. Pain on related joints, 4 weeks** | For every region the new step loads (from its `regions`), the maximum logged pain was **≤2/10** on every set of every exercise loading that region in the last **28 days**. |
| **G3. Morning checks, 2 weeks** | No "worse" morning check for those regions in the last **14 days**. |
| **G4. No recent regressions** | No R7 regression or pause, and no R7 performance-drop event, on that ladder in the last **14 days**. No week-on-week rise in pain flagged for those regions. |
| **G5. Timing** | Programme week ≥ the step's minimum week (table below). It is not a deload week and not the first week after a deload (R8 keeps the same steps that week). |
| **G6. In-app readiness test** | Passed within the last **14 days**, where the table defines a test. The test runs in place of the warm-up "potentiate" set, or as one extra set at the end of the session. To pass: the stated performance at RIR ≥2, pain ≤2/10 on every related region, and the next morning not "worse". If the morning is "worse", the test counts as failed. |

**After a failed test:** retest no sooner than 7 days later.

**After unlocking:** the first 2–3 sessions use R6.3 bridging: one set on the new step, the remaining sets on the previous step. Variations of the new step are not offered for its first 2 sessions (R12).

**No confirmation step (user's choice, October 2026):** all steps unlock through G1–G6 only. For the highest-risk steps (P9, K11, X6, marked "Caution" below), the app shows a caution before the test that recommends a physiotherapist assessment. The tests for these steps also use stricter pain limits (≤1/10).

**How the app records a test** *(implementation)*: the test is offered as one extra item at the end of a session. The user does it, then answers whether the stated performance was reached at the stated reps in reserve and gives the highest pain. The answer is saved as a set with the exercise ID `test:<step ID>` (reps 1 = passed, 0 = not passed). G3 then applies through the next-morning check.

### Exercise variations and advanced steps

Last researched: October 2026.

This section extends "Exercise candidates" in two ways:

- **Variations:** versions of an existing ladder step with about the same difficulty and a different emphasis.
- **Advanced steps:** harder steps appended to the ladders, plus one new ladder (X, crawl and plank movement).

The rotation rule is R12 and the unlock rules are R13 (in "Programme rules derived from the evidence").

**Constraints applied:**
- Mat and wall only.
- Sensitive knees, shoulders and wrists, with no diagnosis.
- No overhead skills and no overhead-loaded moves: no pike push-up, handstand or wall walk.
- No very advanced balance skills: no crow, pistol squat, planche, L-sit, human flag or headstand.
- Advanced steps stay at a level that a non-athlete in mid-life could reach with steady practice.

**Source access:** the PubMed, PMC and publisher pages could not be opened from the research environment. Most details below therefore come from abstracts, indexed summaries and secondary reports. Every figure that could not be checked against the full text is marked **(verify)**. EMG (muscle activity) studies show which muscles an exercise targets in healthy, mostly young people. They do not show training outcomes or effects on pain.

**Legend (as in "Exercise candidates", with one addition):**
- **Knee, Wrist and Shoulder load:** L = low, M = medium, H = high. Shoulder load is a new column. It rates load near end range (horizontal abduction, elevation above 90°, or full bodyweight on one arm), not general effort.
- **Difficulty vs step:** "similar", "harder" or "easier" compared with the standard version of the same step. This rating is used by R12 below. It is mostly *(heuristic)*, because no study matched the difficulty of these bodyweight variations.

## Key uncertainties

1. **No diagnosis.** All knee, shoulder and wrist rules are generic. A physiotherapist assessment could change exercise choices, pain limits and depth limits considerably.
2. **The pain-monitoring thresholds** (Thomeé 1997; Silbernagel 2007) were developed for patellofemoral pain and Achilles tendinopathy. The thresholds are based on clinical experience, and this document uses a stricter limit of 3/10 as a safety margin.
3. **Floor-only back work** does not substitute for pulling exercises in loading the lats or biceps. The supporting evidence for it is EMG data in healthy people, not hypertrophy or clinical trials.
4. **The 2026 ACSM resistance training stand and the 2026 AHA statement** were read only through announcements and summaries. Their exact numbers should be checked.
5. **Bodyweight progression evidence** (Kotarsky 2018) is short-term and in young men.
6. **RIR accuracy** is limited, especially for high-rep sets (Halperin 2022), so the progression rules may need tuning from logged data.
7. **Weekly stretching dose** (Thomas 2018): whether it applies per muscle or as a total is unclear (verify).
8. Several page numbers, DOIs and secondary-reported figures are marked **(verify)** throughout.
9. **EMG is not hypertrophy or pain relief.** Almost all variation evidence is acute surface EMG in small samples (6–40 people) of young, healthy adults, often one session. It shows relative emphasis only.
10. **Difficulty equivalence between variations is untested** for bodyweight work. The "similar / harder / easier" ratings are judgements. Narrow-hand push-ups (more EMG, more elbow torque) are rated harder. Wide-hand push-ups are rated easier because of lower elbow torque (Donkers 1993, secondary summary; verify).
11. **Many details could not be checked against full texts:** Lehecka 2017 figures, Kang 2016 results, Boren 2011 percentages, Youdas 2014, Losavio 2023 percentages, Nunes 2020 figures, and the Escamilla 2008 and 2022 knee-angle ranges.
12. **Side lunges load the patellofemoral joint more than forward lunges** at mid-to-deep knee flexion (Escamilla 2008, 2022). For these knees, K10 and K11 carry the highest knee risk of the new steps. K11 shows a caution before its test for that reason.
13. **No direct evidence was found** for bear crawls, shoulder taps, plank up-downs, push-up to side plank, Spiderman push-ups or hollow rocks. The X ladder and P10/C9 rest on coaching practice and indirect data.
14. **Overhead boundary:** S0f and E5 involve unloaded arm elevation. Because the user excluded overhead work (October 2026), they are capped at shoulder height.
15. **Readiness thresholds** (2 of 3 sessions, 28-day pain window, 14-day morning window, minimum weeks) are design choices. They are not derived from studies. Tune them from logged data.
